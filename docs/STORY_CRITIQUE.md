# Story Critique — the Greyfall Campaign

Adversarial editorial pass on `docs/STORY_BIBLE.md`, round 1 of a two-round
loop. Sources read in full: `docs/CREATIVE_BIBLE.md` (§2 tone, §5 magic, §12
campaign canon), `docs/STORY_BIBLE.md`, `docs/ENCOUNTER_NOTES.md`, the shipped
dialogue in `data/encounters/e1`–`e5`, `docs/CONTENT_NOTES.md` §1.

Severity scale: **STRUCTURAL** (a layer must change) > **SIGNIFICANT** (beats
must change) > **CRAFT** (execution, fixable in place). **[BINDING-CANON]**
marks findings that touch accepted Layers 0–1 canon and therefore escalate to
the project owner rather than being freely rewritable. Every finding cites its
passages; every finding proposes a direction, not a fix — the author owns the
fix.

## Round 1 — Critique

### F1 — STRUCTURAL [BINDING-CANON] — The final battle is a fight the antagonist does not need, against a man the protagonist has never met, at no cost

Three defects converge on the Standards Hall, and together they make the
climax of a tactics game dramatically inert even though it is thematically
correct.

**(a) Gault's stated preference order dissolves the battle's necessity.** The
Chapter 5 summary: Gault "loses the field and wins the outcome he prefers:
the truth received, attested, sealed under standard, and unread." The
character table: "Final antagonist; prefers the truth filed." If filing is the
outcome he *prefers*, the spire assault is a battle whose winner and loser
want the same result — he could open the Archive gate and skip the fight. Beat
5.7 gestures at the correct version ("Gault permits what he could no longer
prevent and prefers it to the alternative"), which implies a preference order
of *no deposition > filing > publication* — but the Layer 1 summary and the
trajectory table both state the collapsed version, and Layer 1 is binding.
One sentence of canon decides whether the final battle exists. Note the fix is
already latent in the document's own Chapter 5 excerpt: "persons unknown
forced the Assay spire and were repelled without loss to the record" — Gault
must be *seen to repel*; the repulsion is itself a filing. Say so, in a
binding sentence.

**(b) Rowen and Gault have never exchanged a word before 5.6.** Audit the
beats: Gault is introduced at 3.9 (one public sentence), speaks to Aldric at
4.3, reads a file alone at 4.10, and then is the final boss. The bible asks
"who is actually across the field and why do they personally matter" of
itself implicitly — the answer is: a thesis statement Rowen met two battles
ago. His mid-battle argument ("Publish, and the amber goes dark in every lamp
in the Underveins by spring") is the campaign's best villain line and it is
wasted on a stranger. Direction: give him one prior contact with Rowen, and
the material is already lying there — the Assay "measured Attunement to issue
licenses" (bible §7) and Layer 3b insists "the Assay measured her once and
the measurement stands." Let Gault be the man who assayed her for her
commission. She is already a number in his archive; make that a scene, not a
subtext.

**(c) The climax has no cost and answers a question the player already
answered.** Beat 5.4 settles the deposit-vs-detonate argument; beat 5.6 has
Gault restate the same argument ("count who starves first" → "the amber goes
dark... by spring") to a player who resolved it two beats earlier. And Chapter
5 is the only chapter of a permadeath campaign in which no named character
dies — 5.8 is a demobilization, not a price. Rowen's true sacrifice (she
spends the only bomb she has by burying it) is real but it is signed in 5.4
and merely executed thereafter. Direction: move some of the ethical weight
*into* the battle (the 5.5 brown-out choice is exactly right — extend that
logic upward), and consider whether the spire costs the company a named body.

The ending's register — filed, not published; erased but alive — is binding,
correct, and untouched by this finding. The problem is not the destination;
it is that the last three hours of play are a procession toward it.

### F2 — STRUCTURAL — Rowen receives the plot; the evidentiary chain is a bucket brigade

The prompt under audit: does Rowen drive the plot or witness it. Chapter by
chapter, the campaign's load-bearing discoveries are *handed to her*:

- 3.1 — Maud's letter "reaches Rowen through Tally."
- 3.4 — Lunn "hands it over and vanishes rather than testify."
- 3.5 — Nessa "gives Rowen the question, not the answer."
- 4.5 — Aldric summons *her* and hands over the Sedge portfolio ledger.
- 4.8 — Quill "surrenders the charter's sealed annex."
- 5.3 — Jory opens the door: "Jory's one unsigned act."

Six donations. Against them, Rowen genuinely earns three things: the
pay-paper at the Bonding Sheds (3.2), the exhibit list (2.8), and the trench
face (4.1) — and even 4.1 happens because *Edda Marle* challenged the
interdict and Rowen's company is "embedded with a Marle survey crew." Chapter
4 — the campaign's hinge — is moved by Aldric (columns), Gault (stewardship),
Marle (the challenge), and the Assay (the interments); Rowen's trajectory
entry for it reads "Opens the trench; takes the annex," and the second verb is
false — the annex is given. This is FFT's chapters 3–4 disease imported intact
along with the structure the bible licenses: Ramza wandering, collecting
stones, while Delita politics; Rowen wandering, collecting documents, while
Jory politics. Homage at the structure level has here traced the original's
*flaw*.

Her genuine choices — declining the clean name (3.7), deposit-not-detonate
(5.4), and the desertion and coat-pocket of shipped canon — are excellent, and
Chapter 2's shape (she acts, the world defeats her actions) is legitimate
tragedy. The middle is the problem. Direction: convert at least two donations
into extractions with cost — the annex is the obvious one (Quill's surrender
becomes something Rowen must take, or trade for, at a price the archive
exacts); and give the Pump House (4.9), currently a forced loss ("saves what
it carries and loses the rest"), a player-facing triage choice of *which*
truth to save. That is agency in the story's own vocabulary.

### F3 — STRUCTURAL — Jory's rise happens inside a time skip; the Delita engine has no onscreen combustion

Layer 1 declares "Jory Slate's rise → Chapter 3" as an engine. Audit what the
player actually sees. Chapter 2: she testifies (a thing done *to* her — "the
panel... corrects her recollection") and takes the clerkship (one good scene,
one great line). Then "about two years on," Chapter 3 opens and she has
*already risen*: the Act is already "the Compact's public argument," and her
entire political method is disposed of in one narrated clause — "she plays
the houses that fund reform to weaken each other." Told, never staged. Her
on-page Chapter 3 beats are the lodge argument (her position, stated once),
being the *target* at the Grange (protected, unaware), and the night platform.
All three are reactive or static. Delita works in FFT because the player
*watches* him seize, deceive, and kill onscreen — ruthlessness witnessed, not
reported. Jory's ruthlessness is entirely in the summary layer, and Appendix B
confesses the wound: "a Jory interlude chapter is possible between 3 and 4 and
deliberately unplanned," and "the Ch2–Ch3 time skip's texture" is "left
open."

Direction: at minimum, one beat per chapter (2, 3, 4) in which the player
watches Jory do something ruthless-and-defensible to a person we know — a
witness she declines to protect, a lodge demand she trades away on-screen, a
name she lets the record correct — so that co-signing the stewardship compact
in 5.1 is the last step of a staircase we climbed with her, not a fact about
an offscreen career. If the interlude chapter is the fix, commit to it; if
not, Layer 2 must carry the rise.

### F4 — SIGNIFICANT [BINDING-CANON] — The ending's moral premise is asserted, never demonstrated

The whole ending stands on one counterfactual: "publish and the amber goes
dark in every Underveins lamp by spring" (5.4, restated by Gault in 5.6).
Layer 0 calls it "the winners' one true premise" — *true* by authorial fiat.
The campaign never shows the mechanism: no beat anywhere in Chapters 2–5
demonstrates what truth-leakage actually does to an amber market, a lamp
price, a street. Marek's "You don't blow a gas main to prove the gas is
there" is a metaphor standing where evidence should be. Since Rowen's keystone
choice — and the binding ending's entire moral weight — rests on this premise,
it must be earned in play. Direction: one beat, probably Chapter 4, where a
local leak of the pit-rows truth (a rumor, a walked-off render crew, a
lamp-boycott, a renderer beaten in the Row) produces a small, visible,
starving-first consequence the company witnesses. The ending does not change;
it stops being a syllogism and becomes a memory. Flagged [BINDING-CANON]
because it supplies the load test for Layer 0's keystone, though the fix
itself is additive at Layer 2.

### F5 — SIGNIFICANT — Why Rowen is allowed to live is characterized for the House and hand-waved for the Assay

Chapters 1–3 survive the question: the hunter is the House, Aldric's weapon is
explicitly the record, not the knife ("You will not be in the record, Rowen.
You will be in the refinery" — e5), and Dray's warrant for "unidentified
parties" is the House hunting her deniably. That is characterized and it
holds. But from 4.10 the Assay *knows* — "the Sodality learns that the dead
ensign is alive and holds the annex" — and its total response is one elegant
line ("She is dead in the record. That is a fact about the record") plus one
raid aimed at *records*, not at her (4.9). Meanwhile the same machine kills
Aldric "within days" for learning *less*, by columns. A Seat is harder to
kill than a woman with no legal existence — killing the officially dead is the
safest murder in the Compact, since there is no record to disturb. As written,
the campaign's most dangerous person is protected by the villain's best
epigram.

Direction — two compatible options: (a) make the doctrine-blindness
explicitly load-bearing, i.e. state that the same rule that cannot see a
person outside the record is the rule that cannot refuse a filing — Gault's
hamartia and the deposition's mechanism are then *one fact*, and the hole
becomes the theme; (b) arm Rowen with stated leverage after 4.8 (distributed
contingent copies of the annex — a dead-hand switch the Assay can price). The
material for (a) is already on the page; it is never said.

### F6 — SIGNIFICANT — R1 is solved by the player two chapters before the story stages its reveal, and beat 3.5 hands over the conspiracy

Beat 2.6 puts three facts in one battle: a walled gallery with "dead in it,"
vein-glass "glowing through" that same gallery, and "the Assay quietly buys
the worked-out ground" in the same chapter. That is not a plant; it is the
answer, assembled. Then 3.4 (richest fields ruled impure) confirms it, and 3.5
overreaches: Nessa's "The impurity is in what it's drawn *from*. And they
know what it's drawn from" states both the secret and the concealment out
loud, a full chapter before 4.1. The ladder's own audit ("the player can
assemble R1 from shown material alone by 4.1. Pass") measures the wrong
event — assembly *by* the reveal is fine; assembly at 2.6–3.5 means 4.1 is
staged as a revelation ("nobody says the sentence aloud in this beat" — the
restraint of a surprise) for a player who has none left, and restraint reads
as coyness. To be fair: "the church did it" is assumed by every genre-literate
player from the pitch, and the story rightly makes 2–4's tension *what*, not
*who*. The failure is local and fixable. Direction: own the earliness — stage
4.1 as confirmation whose horror is scale and specificity (soldiers in column;
her family's war), not fact; cut 3.5's second sentence ("And they know...")
and let Nessa's question stay a question; consider whether 2.6 needs all three
facts in one beat or whether the Assay purchase can move to 3.x.

### F7 — SIGNIFICANT — The reveal ladder fails its own audit rule twice

The ladder's stated rule: "no reveal lands without at least two earlier
plants, at least one of them shown."

- **R5 (the founding knew) has zero shown plants.** Its two listed plants are
  "3.9 Gault... (*told*, as liturgy)" and "4.3... 'your grandfather signed
  the annex' (*told, politically*)." Both told, by the document's own
  annotation — the rule is violated on its face. Worse, 4.3 is not a plant;
  it *is* the reveal, spoken aloud five beats before 4.8 shows the paper. As
  structured, R5 has one oblique pre-reveal plant (a liturgical sentence no
  player will bank) and a reveal split into "told at 4.3, shown at 4.8."
  Direction: either add a genuine shown plant before 4.3 (the framers'
  foreknowledge made visible — an ownership chain on a boundary stone, the
  annex's seal glimpsed in Maud's papers), or reclassify 4.3 as the reveal
  and let 4.8 be its exhibit.
- **R2's plants support the wrong content.** The reveal's *new* information
  at 3.2 is multiplicity — "one broker, three cities, three houses," a
  product line. Every listed plant (e1's professional gate-swing, e3's "a
  month of shipments," 2.2's warrant wording) supports what Chapter 1 already
  revealed: that the provocateurs were professionals in Corvane's frame-up.
  Nothing before 3.2 points at *other cities or other houses*. By the rule's
  spirit, the reveal's actual surprise has zero plants. Direction: one shown
  plant of elsewhere — a Saltmere stencil in a Chapter 2 beat, a provocateur
  the company half-recognizes working a different city's colors.
- **R4 is marginal:** the letter proves Rowen alive at +3 years; the reveal
  is +30 and "the old woman." Only "her hand, present-tense care" carries the
  distance. Acceptable for a soft reveal; noted, not pressed.
- For the record, **R3 (Tam Ash) passes and is earned, not merely clever**:
  "a wife on Tallow Row and a son in the yard" (e2, shipped), plus the
  annotator's voice-tells (writes "the pour"; restores Exhibit 19 "with
  disproportionate care") are real plants doing real work, and the reveal
  pays the campaign's oldest debt (the unlogged name). Its one risk: Tam is a
  motif, not a person — the player has never met him — so the reveal lands on
  Perren's ghost, which is fine, but do not expect it to land on Tam.

### F8 — SIGNIFICANT — The texture restates what the spine advances: one irony, told twenty-five times

The frame track genuinely progresses (opens → widens → writes the future →
inverts), and Chapter 3's "the record has begun writing the future" (3.10) and
Chapter 4's inversion are real advances. The beat level is another matter.
Count the record-vs-truth restatements: five shipped battle beats ("an
unidentified party leaving the scene," "we log numbers, ma'am," "that is a
paperwork matter," "the two documents are not required to agree," "you will
be in the refinery"), plus — Chapter 2 alone — the corrected testimony, the
burned docket, the medal ("They gave it to her. She's theirs"), the warrant's
wording, and the published Finding; plus five chapter-end excerpt/annotation
pairs each performing the identical move. The log-the-name motif alone runs
four full statements: Perren unlogged (e2) → Exhibit 19 (Ch1 annotation) →
the lodge book (4.7) → Dray dying on "Log it" (4.6). By 4.6 the player has
been able to write Dray's death-line since battle two — his entire arc is one
ironic rhyme announced four battles in advance. And five of the campaign's
battles are the same story-shape: fight to obtain, move, or save paper (Mercy
Hall crates, Records Annex list, Bonding Sheds pay-paper, Pump House records,
plus the Tallow Row docket) — the theme has one prop. Tragedies that repeat
one irony flatten, and the bible knows this ("The split, stated once, cleanly,
and never restaged" — 3.3 shows the document *has* the discipline; it just
doesn't apply it to its central irony). Direction: budget the motif — cap
record-vs-truth beats per chapter, cut or complicate the weakest instances
(Dray's death-line is the one to make *swerve*: let him ask for something the
player did not predict), and let at least two mid-campaign reveals live in a
non-paper medium (a body, a machine, a place — 4.1 proves the story can do
it).

### F9 — SIGNIFICANT — Maren is instrumentalized into exactly the statistic the story condemns

Her active beats: one choice ("The steward stays where the members can find
her" — 2.4, her best moment) and one argument (3.3). After that she is
function: "rebuilds the lodges" (one narrated clause), is diagnosed as "a
projected compensable case" (3.10), "dies; first posthumous award" (4.7), and
becomes a handoff (Della takes the book). The beat 3.10 irony — the record
pricing her death before she dies — is brilliant, but it cuts both ways: the
*plot's* use of Maren is identical to the record's, a projected compensable
loss booked three chapters ahead, and the story never notices the
resemblance. She does not exist *only* to die — 2.4 is real — but everything
after 3.3 exists only to make her death maximally useful to Jory's cost, the
lodge book, and the Act's irony. Direction: one beat of unbudgeted life —
something Maren does in Chapter 3 or 4 that neither the theme nor another
character's arc needs, ideally something that *fails to fit* the lodge book's
tidy afterlife.

### F10 — SIGNIFICANT — Dray is overloaded, and his one irreversible choice is unmotivated on the page

One sergeant carries: the procedural voice of all five shipped battles, the
Chapter 2 hunter, the Chapter 3 disgrace, the Chapter 4 boss, the Augmented
job's tragedy made flesh (Layer 3b: "his boss sheet in 4.6 should *be* his
tragedy"), and the closing statement of the log-the-name motif. The load is
almost bearable — he is a strong device — except at the hinge: "Dray,
disgraced cornering the company on the Assay's own metering floor, volunteers
for the graft program" (Ch3 summary; beat 3.8 gives it half a clause more).
The campaign's most procedure-bound man electing body-horror is a major
character event disposed of in a subordinate clause, offscreen, between
beats. FFT gave Wiegraf a whole descending arc before the stone; Dray gets a
sentence. And spending him as the Chapter 4 boss leaves Chapter 5 with no
personal antagonist at all (see F1b). Direction: give the volunteering one
scene — what the procedure-man tells himself when the procedure discards
him — and consider whether Dray's fall belongs one chapter later, or whether
some warm body from his story should stand in the spire.

### F11 — SIGNIFICANT — The burned-docket beat is internally muddled about what burned, and the annotation's flagship claim rests on the muddle

Checked against shipped canon first: there is **no contradiction with e5** —
in e3 the docket goes "in my coat," and in e5 Rowen names it and keeps it ("I
did not come to say it. I came to keep the docket"); nothing shipped lays it
before Aldric physically. The real problem is inside Chapter 2's own text.
Beat 2.7: "Tally brokers **the docket** to the Third Commissioner... it is
examined, declared forged, and burned... **The original never left her
coat.**" Both sentences cannot be straightforwardly true. If copies were
tendered, then the Inquiry's "documents of doubtful provenance" ruling was —
for once — technically *correct* (they were uncertified reproductions), which
undercuts the Chapter 2 annotation's flagship line "The forgeries were not
forged," and undercuts the lesson Rowen draws ("appealing ends and archiving
begins") since she never actually risked the original. If the original was
tendered and burned, the epilogue's parcel ("A docket, decades old, Corvane
Freight stencil") cannot exist. Direction: decide the instrument — the clean
version is a *certified* copy in Hessle's own registered hand (clerks always
keep a copy cuts both ways), so the burning destroys something with real
standing and the annotation's claim survives on the counterfoil books.
Adjacent and worth one clause somewhere: Wick — the man who said "we've been
carrying your name up this street for a month" in front of a Watch sergeant —
is bought out of Watch custody by the Ledger in Chapter 2, and the House that
should want him silenced lets it happen unremarked.

### F12 — SIGNIFICANT — The frame collects its benefits and pays its costs only where they don't hurt

Credit first, because it is real: the frame's discipline on unknowables is the
document's signature move — Nessa "never seen again and never confirmed dead,"
Aldric's death "filed... open" (R7), "the ambiguity is the horror." Those are
costs paid. But the frame's two expensive costs go unpaid:

- **Pre-litigation.** Every chapter ends with the annotator explaining the
  irony the player just experienced, and repeatedly *outgunning* the player
  with evidence the player never touches: the counterfoil books (Ch2
  annotation), the bier-dockets (Ch4 annotation). The player's discoveries
  arrive second-hand to a narrator who already owns better proof; the
  archivist functions as a laugh track for ironies and demotes the player
  from investigator to reader. Direction: make the annotations withhold and
  ask rather than resolve — Tam should lack at least one thing only the
  player saw (he nearly does: the miners' sign, the arithmetic scene), and
  should say so; and at least one annotation should be *wrong* and corrected
  later, so his authority is earned rather than issued.
- **Source discipline breaks in private rooms.** If the whole game is the
  merged record — Inquiry, Quill's duplicates, Rowen's deposition and
  letters — then 4.10 (Gault alone, reading a file, speaking to no one) and
  arguably 4.3 (Gault–Aldric alone; Aldric dead within days, partially
  recoverable via the 4.5 handover) have no attestable source. Either mark
  such scenes as the annotator's conjecture — a mode the frame never uses
  and would be *strengthened* by — or give them sources. A frame this proud
  of provenance cannot exempt its villain's best scenes from it.
- Also under this heading: the frame plus the loss condition (`unitDowned:
  rowen`, shipped) means the player holds a survival guarantee from battle
  one; the story knows (FFT had the same) but should stop *also* spending
  suspense beats on whether Rowen will be taken (2.2's "Rowen must not be
  taken or identified" reads as jeopardy the frame has already voided —
  restate such stakes as costs to others, which 2.2 half does).

### F13 — CRAFT — Derivativeness: two places where rhyme slips toward tracing

The homage is licensed (bible §3: "structure level, never the surface") and
mostly honored — the vein-glass secret is a genuinely different *kind* of
secret from the Zodiac stones (the dead as feedstock vs. demons), the
boardroom-for-throne-room swap works, and filed-not-published is a true
inversion of the Durai papers (FFT's truth is seized and suppressed; Rowen
*chooses* the sealing — protect that difference at all costs, it is the
campaign's claim to originality). Two spots cross the line:

- **Chapters 3–4 transpose FFT's weakest stretch beat-for-beat**: protagonist
  roams collecting artifacts-of-truth while the commoner friend politics
  offscreen and the church accumulates. A player who knows FFT will
  experience the *shape* of the middle as a retread — and, per F2, the shape
  imports the original's passivity disease. Fixing F2/F3 fixes this for free;
  flagged separately so the fix is credited to intent rather than accident.
- **The epilogue's mechanics** — annotating historian, suppressed truth,
  final-line vindication of the erased hero — are the Durai report's beats in
  order. The inversion (deposit vs. suppression; "no date of death" vs.
  posthumous rehabilitation) saves it, but only if the epilogue *leans on*
  the inversion; as drafted, Scene 3's furniture (the lone archivist, the
  parcel, the amendment) is furniture FFT owns. Direction: let the epilogue
  spend a beat on what is *different* — Tam amends the record as an act the
  Sodality's own doctrine cannot refuse, the machine turned on itself —
  rather than on the reveal choreography FFT already performed.

### F14 — CRAFT — Tone: the register holds, but everyone speaks in the same lapidary voice, and two beats drift sentimental

No doomsday drift found anywhere — stakes stay political and economic through
the finale, and that discipline deserves to be named. The drift is
elsewhere:

- **Aphorism monoculture.** Gault: "Standards do not sit. They stand under
  everything" / "Records accumulate" / "A tolerance is not a lie." Aldric:
  "a confession with a yield curve" / "Where a thing is signed is not where
  it happens." Jory: "go be a paragraph in their document." Tally: "That
  costs extra to ignore." Marek: "You don't blow a gas main..." Rowen: "the
  only kind of open I trust." Every named speaker, both registers, delivers
  the same rhetorical shape — short declarative plus turn. The bible's §5.5
  two-register rule governs *vocabulary*; nothing yet differentiates
  *rhetoric*, so the cast risks sounding like one very good writer. (It
  starts in shipped canon — Rowen aphorizes in battle one: "Height is a thing
  you can be given and a thing you can be taken off.") Direction: assign each
  major voice one non-lapidary habit (Jory runs long when she's lying; Marek
  never abstracts; Gault alone earns the epigram) and let Gault's polish
  stand out *because* the others lack it.
- **Maud's posthumous letter** (3.1) — the secretly-knowing dead mother with
  a lawyer-held letter is the story's one melodrama-catalog device, and it
  doubles as a plot courier (it supplies the 4.5 summons channel). It is the
  least Greyfall beat in the document. Direction: keep the channel, lose the
  clairvoyance — let the letter be ambiguous business paper the annotator
  reads too much into, which is on-theme.
- **Scene 3's stage directions** lean lyrical against §2's "unsentimental,
  dry": "the way you smooth a thing you will never move again"; "the dark
  that used to be a seam" (used twice). Once each, maybe; twice is the
  author savoring. Scene 2's "Log me as nobody" flirts with a curtain-line
  quip (and an Odyssey echo) — it survives, barely, because the clerk's log
  is literal; watch it in revision.

### F15 — CRAFT — Timeline pressure: the arithmetic closes, but only just, and always offscreen

- **Jory:** foundry hand at Ch1 → Inquiry witness weeks later → clerkship →
  "about two years on," the Compact's landmark Act is already "the Compact's
  public argument" with her as architect, in a federation run by hereditary
  houses, with proscribed-union origins. Two years is aggressive even before
  noting (F3) that all of it happens between chapters. The Ch3 excerpt's "two
  years' patient work" at least matches the clerkship's start.
- **The letter:** the Ch3 annotation files "a letter in R.C.'s hand, dated
  three years after her recorded death" that prices the Act — so the Act
  passes ≥3 years post-Ch1, meaning Chapter 3 must internally span close to a
  year after opening "about two years on." Workable; state it once so a
  reader doesn't do this arithmetic against you.
- **The old woman:** Rowen is the House's youngest, newly commissioned, at
  Ch1 — call it 19–20; the campaign runs ~4–5 years; +30 puts her middle
  fifties at the epilogue. "An old woman in a canvas coat" is defensible for
  a rail-work life in this world, but one more campaign-year or one fewer
  claim of youth would remove the squint.

## The three findings I would die on

1. **F1.** As canonized, the final battle of a tactics game is a fight the
   antagonist prefers to lose, against a stranger, at no cost. One binding
   sentence about Gault's preference order, one earlier scene between Gault
   and Rowen, and a real price inside Chapter 5 turn a thematically correct
   ending into a playable one. Until then the campaign's last hours are a
   procession, and no amount of register discipline will hide it.

2. **F2.** Six of the campaign's nine decisive pieces of evidence are handed
   to Rowen by other people's choices. The story's own thesis — she is the
   counter-record's *author* — is contradicted by a middle in which she is
   its recipient. The bible licensed FFT's structure and accidentally shipped
   FFT's disease.

3. **F3.** Jory's rise is the Chapter 3 engine and it happens entirely inside
   a time skip, her ruthlessness confined to one narrated clause. A Delita
   arc the player never watches climb is not an arc; it is a rumor. The
   document already knows this — the "deliberately unplanned" interlude in
   Appendix B is the tell — and the decision cannot stay deferred, because
   every Jory beat in Chapters 3–5 is priced by whether we saw her pay.

## What the story does best

The document's rarest virtue is its discipline about unknowable things, and it
must not be sanded off in revision: Aldric's death filed open ("accidents are
not this tidy" and nothing more, R7's "reveal: none — binding"), Nessa's fate
held as "a gap the annotator marks and cannot fill," the annex's October
arithmetic "deliberately never resolved" — a campaign that refuses to know
things is the only kind that earns a frame device about the limits of the
record. Second, the mechanics-story weave in Layer 3b is the best
tactics-narrative integration I have seen on paper: "the dead stay dead" as
the *reason vein-glass exists*, Vale's licence revocation as a number that
